# Common Attack Vectors

## Course Overview

An attack vector is the method or path an attacker uses to gain unauthorized access to a system or data. This material gives an overview of three of the most common categories: phishing, malware, and social engineering.

## Phishing

Phishing is an attempt to trick someone into revealing sensitive information (like passwords or credit card numbers) or clicking a malicious link, usually through a message that looks like it's from a trustworthy source.

Common signs of a phishing attempt:

- An urgent or threatening tone ("your account will be locked in 24 hours")
- A sender address that looks similar to, but not exactly, a legitimate one
- A link that, when hovered over, points to an unexpected or unfamiliar domain

Spear phishing is a more targeted version, where the attacker researches a specific person or organization to make the message more convincing.

## Malware

Malware ("malicious software") is any software designed to damage, disrupt, or gain unauthorized access to a system. Common types include:

- **Viruses**: attach themselves to legitimate files and spread when those files are shared or run
- **Ransomware**: encrypts a victim's files and demands payment for the decryption key
- **Spyware**: secretly collects information about a user's activity
- **Trojans**: disguise themselves as legitimate software to trick users into installing them

Malware often spreads through infected email attachments, malicious downloads, or compromised websites.

## Social Engineering

Social engineering is the broader practice of manipulating people — rather than exploiting technical vulnerabilities — into breaking normal security procedures. Phishing is one form of social engineering, but it also includes tactics like:

- **Pretexting**: creating a fabricated scenario (e.g., impersonating IT support) to extract information
- **Baiting**: leaving an infected USB drive somewhere it's likely to be found and plugged in out of curiosity
- **Tailgating**: following an authorized person through a secured door without their own access badge

## Why This Matters

Many successful attacks don't rely on breaking encryption or exploiting complex software bugs — they rely on tricking a person. Recognizing these patterns is one of the most effective defenses, since even the strongest technical security can be undermined by a single successful social engineering attempt.
